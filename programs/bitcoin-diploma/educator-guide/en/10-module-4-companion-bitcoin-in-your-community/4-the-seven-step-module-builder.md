# 10.4 The seven-step module builder

#### 1. Learn the local context

Investigate:

* currencies and units of account;
* common cash, bank, mobile-money, and card payment methods;
* internet, electricity, data, and device reliability;
* banking and identity access;
* common wage, merchant, donation, or remittance patterns;
* Bitcoin knowledge and tool availability;
* language and accessibility needs;
* fraud, coercion, surveillance, and physical-safety risks;
* legal, tax, accounting, consumer, and reporting context; and
* available technical or community support.

Do not ask learners to reveal personal financial details to complete this research. Use public information, voluntary community consultation, and invented cases.

#### 2. Define the local need or opportunity

Write one sentence:

> Learners will examine whether Bitcoin can help ________ by comparing or demonstrating ________ under the local conditions of ________.

If the sentence starts with a product name, return to the need.

#### 3. Select a focused module

Choose one primary task and, at most, one supporting task for a minimum module. Suitable areas may include:

* receiving payment for work;
* an everyday purchase;
* family remittance or cross-border payment comparison;
* merchant payment and refund flow;
* community or nonprofit donation;
* on-ramp and off-ramp comparison;
* Lightning payment where appropriate;
* local resource or merchant discovery;
* circular-economy mapping;
* local money-problem analysis; or
* another approved locally relevant Bitcoin-as-money task.

Every certification task must map to both `B4.1` and `B4.2` through an approved variant.

#### 4. Verify facts, tools, and expertise

For every tool or service, record:

* exact name and version;
* publisher and verified source;
* custody model;
* supported network and payment requests;
* setup, funding, and recovery path;
* fees, spreads, limits, and liquidity constraints;
* data collected and privacy consequences;
* compatibility and device requirements;
* failure and support process;
* ownership or policy changes;
* local legal or tax relevance;
* alternative if the tool fails; and
* date checked.

Invite a qualified local expert when needed. Brief them on the objectives, no-promotion boundary, privacy rules, and assessment role. The educator remains responsible for the learning environment.

#### 5. Design Presentation → Practice → Production

**Presentation:** Demonstrate the complete local path, including the non-Bitcoin alternative, costs, custody, failure points, and stop conditions.

**Practice:** Learners rehearse with printed materials, a simulation, test environment, or approved low-stakes setup.

**Production:** Learners complete the defined task or direct its decisions with only permitted support, then explain the relevant trade-offs.

#### 6. Define safety and evidence

Before class, specify:

* exact objective and approved variant;
* observable action sequence;
* required verification behavior;
* required explanation;
* permitted prompt level;
* privacy and consent boundaries;
* safety-critical errors;
* offline equivalent;
* **Meets standard** and **Not yet**; and
* evidence record and reassessment route.

#### 7. Debrief and choose a safe next step

Ask learners to identify:

* the local need;
* the existing alternative;
* the Bitcoin path;
* who controls the keys and records at each stage;
* total costs and time;
* strongest benefit;
* most serious risk or failure point;
* locally specific rule or condition;
* evidence observed; and
* smallest responsible next step.

The next step may be further learning, another simulation, checking a current rule, seeking qualified help, or deciding not to use the path.
